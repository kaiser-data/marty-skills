# Front-Loading Findings

## Overview

A short version is not a compressed long version. It is a different document with a
fixed number of slots, filled by what the work established, with everything else moved
to an appendix and cited from the body.

**Core principle: a slot in the front is earned by a finding, not by narrative order.
Nothing is deleted — unearned material is demoted and cited, and a demoted exhibit that
nothing cites was cut without anyone deciding to cut it.**

Not for: a venue that wants full detail, or when the long document is itself the
deliverable. Then the work is editing, not budgeting.

## The front is a budget

Write the budget before touching the text. Slots, not pages of prose:

| Slot | What fills it | Earns its place by |
|---|---|---|
| Abstract (at the cap) | the claim, its number, the control that licenses it | being repeatable from memory |
| Framing (~1 p.) | what the work established, and the one sentence that makes it matter | naming the achievement in the first two sentences |
| Method (~1 p.) | the design and the comparison it licenses | being the minimum needed to read the results |
| Results (~2 p.) | the primary table, plus the 2–3 figures that carry findings | each exhibit carrying a *different* finding |
| Secondary result (~1 p.) | the one other thing worth the space | standing alone if the primary is disputed |
| Discussion (~0.5 p.) | what is new here versus prior work | being checkable against that prior work |
| Required headings | whatever the venue mandates | existing at all |
| Appendix (uncounted) | everything demoted | being cited from the body |

The budget is fixed first. Content competes for slots; slots do not expand for content.

## The two moves

**1. Order by achievement, not by origin.** The reader meets the result before the road
to it. Motivation shrinks to the sentence that makes the finding matter, and the gap you
started from becomes a clause rather than a section. If the first page still describes
what you set out to do rather than what you now know, the ordering is still the
lab-notebook ordering.

**2. Demote and cite.** Every demoted element leaves a pointer where it stood: "full
triad in Appendix Table 4", "per-model values in Appendix B". The pointer is what makes
demotion honest — it tells the reader the evidence exists and where. Finish by checking
every appendix label for a citation in the body.

## The triage pass

1. List every exhibit — figure, table, section — on one line each: **which single
   finding does this carry?** An exhibit that needs two lines is two exhibits.
2. Two exhibits, one finding → the clearer one stays, the other is demoted. This is the
   most common cut and the one people resist hardest.
3. Process exhibits — pipeline diagrams, apparatus, prompt listings — go to the
   appendix. Process is not a finding; rigor earns credit in the method paragraph and
   the repository, not a body figure.
4. An exhibit whose finding is already in a table stays only if the picture says
   something the table cannot.
5. Rank what remains by "would a reader repeat this sentence to someone else?" Keep down
   to the budget; demote the rest.
6. Check the venue's required headings against the **short** document. It must carry
   every mandated section even when the long one never had it.

## Dense, not noisy

Density comes from removing connective tissue, never from shrinking type or margins.

- **Claim first.** Every paragraph opens with a bold claim sentence, support following
  in the same paragraph. A reader who reads only the bold sentences should come away
  with the results.
- **A number belongs in the body if a reader would repeat it**; the rest live in the
  tables. One memorable contrast beats five accurate ones.
- **Captions take the same shape:** bold claim, then how to read the figure.
- **Supporting detail becomes a reference.** "(Appendix C)" in place of three sentences
  of caveat. Caveats that change the interpretation stay; caveats that only display
  diligence get demoted.
- **Cut transitions, not content.** "Having established X, we now turn to Y" is a slot
  spent on nothing.

## Quick reference

| Stays in the front | Demoted and cited |
|---|---|
| The exhibit that makes the headline number make sense | A second exhibit telling the same story |
| The one table a reader will actually check | Per-model, per-condition, per-prompt tables |
| A finding a reader would repeat aloud | Pipeline and process diagrams |
| A control the claim depends on | Controls that only rule out exotic alternatives |
| Venue-required headings | Prompt listings, ledgers, raw counts, extended limitations |

## Common mistakes

- **Deleting instead of demoting.** The appendix costs nothing and protects the claim.
- **Shrinking type and margins to fit.** The budget is slots; area is not the constraint.
- **Keeping both exhibits because both are good.** Good is not the test; carrying a
  distinct finding is.
- **An abstract that narrates the method.** At the cap, state the claim, its number, and
  the control — not the procedure that produced them.
- **Softening or upgrading while shortening.** A short version reorders and demotes; it
  does not turn a provisional result into a demonstrated one or drop a hedge for space.
  Where the short and long versions disagree, that is a new error, not compression.
- **A short version that silently supersedes the long one.** Say which is archival.

## Red flags

- "Both figures are good, let us keep both."
- An appendix element that appears nowhere in the body text.
- The first page still explains what you set out to do.
- A required heading that exists in neither document.
