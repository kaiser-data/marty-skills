# Announcing Events

Event posts fail differently from other posts. A missing venue line or a bolded speaker name is not a style flaw — it costs a real person an invitation or a notification. Treat every named human and every logistical fact as load-bearing.

## First: read the organization's last post

Before drafting, pull up the host organization's own most recent post about an event.
Your post will appear beside it in the same feeds, read by the same people. Copy its
conventions deliberately — they are what the community already recognizes.

Harvest specifically:

- **The title line format** — flag/emoji, org × venue, edition name. Reuse it verbatim.
- **The speaker block shape** — emoji per talk, where the name sits, whether talks carry
  a one-line description.
- **Which hashtags they use** and how many.
- **Their standing CTAs** — "interested in speaking or sponsoring, DM me", "follow us
  for the next one". These are reusable and most drafts forget them.

**Do not copy what a recap earns that an announcement hasn't.** Recaps carry attendance
numbers, takeaway lines per talk and a favourite quote — all facts that exist only
*after* the event. Writing a talk description before the talk happens is inventing it.
Announce the title and the speaker; leave the takeaway for the recap.

## The one rule that breaks silently

**Names stay plain. Titles get styled.**

Bold/italic Unicode glyphs are not letters. A bolded name is invisible to @-mention
autocomplete, so the person you meant to notify never learns they were named — and you
find out only after posting, when they don't reshare.

This bites hardest exactly where the urge to style is strongest: the speaker list.

**The recipe — a talk is two lines, never one:**

```
▍**Local-first Agent Teams Running on Your Desktop**
Ryan Zheng
```

Title bold with a `▍` marker; name plain, alone on the next line. The name gains
prominence from the line break, not from the alphabet — and stays taggable.

REQUIRED BACKGROUND: the glyph mechanics, per-alphabet costs and the umlaut/`ß` traps
live in the `linkedin-unicode-styling` skill. This skill covers only what event posts do
with them.

## Two levels of hierarchy, never three

| Level | Style | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Section headers | small caps | 1× |
| Talk titles | bold + `▍` marker | 2× |
| Everything else | plain | 1× |

If a number elsewhere in the post is also bold, bold now means two things and the
hierarchy is gone. Pick one: bold titles **or** bold the single key number. Not both.

## The facts an announcement must carry

Check each against the live event page **on the day you post**, never from memory or
from an earlier draft. Agendas and speaker lineups are routinely filled in late.

- [ ] Date **and** weekday **and** start–end time
- [ ] Venue with street address (a room name alone is not findable)
- [ ] How to register — link the ticket page, do not quote prices
- [ ] Every speaker: name spelled as the event page spells it, plus talk title
- [ ] Co-organizers by name
- [ ] Your own role, stated plainly

**Prices belong on the ticket page, not in the post.** They differ per tier, they
change, and a wrong price in a host's post is a correction thread.

**Registration link goes in the first comment**, not the body — LinkedIn throttles
posts carrying outbound links.

## Role decides what you may promise

| Your role | You may write |
|---|---|
| Host / organizer | Promises about the evening: "bring your problem, I'll find you someone" |
| Speaker | Promises about your talk only |
| Attendee | Recommendation and enthusiasm — no promises |

An attendee promising to introduce people is promising something that isn't theirs.

## Never rank yourself below the people you credit

Naming your co-organizers is generous. Adding a sentence explaining that you learned
everything from them is not — it turns a credit line into a disclaimer, and the
disclaimer is the part readers remember.

```
❌ Most of what I know about running one of these, I learned watching them.
❌ They have run these evenings far longer than I have.
❌ I'm new at this, so I'm mostly improvising.
❌ I'm just helping out.

✅ Looking forward to putting on a great evening with experienced co-hosts.
```

Same warmth, no self-demotion. Credit the people, look forward to the work, stop.

**The test:** would you write this sentence about a colleague? If it only ever points
down at yourself, cut it. This applies to the whole post, not just the co-host block —
these sentences cluster wherever you feel you haven't earned the role yet.

**Praise the others in the third person if you want to praise them:** "an experienced
crew" credits them without measuring yourself against them.

## Before posting: the tag list

Everyone named in the body gets @-mentioned when you post. Write the list explicitly
in the draft file — co-organizers, every speaker, the venue partner, the community page.

Someone named but not tagged hears about it from a third party. That is worse than
not naming them.

Let the platform's autocomplete supply each handle; never hand-type one. If a name
won't autocomplete, check it isn't styled.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | What happens |
|---|---|
| Speaker names bolded with their titles | Tagging silently dies; speakers never notified |
| Agenda copied from an earlier draft | Times or speakers wrong; four co-organizers see it at once |
| Price quoted in the body | Wrong for some tier; needs a correction comment |
| Organization's stats padding a personal post | Reads as a press release; the personal "why" gets buried |
| Registration link in the body | Reach throttled |
| Naming someone, then not tagging them | They learn from someone else |
| "I learned it all from them" under the co-host list | Credit becomes a disclaimer; that's the line readers keep |
| Community founders / history in a host's announcement | Off-topic for an announcement; give it its own post |

## Scope discipline

An announcement answers: what, when, where, who, how do I get in, why should I care.

Community history, founding stories, membership numbers and chapter counts are a
*different post*. They are usually true and usually researched — which is exactly why
they survive edits they shouldn't. Cut them, and leave a note in the draft saying where
the research went, so nobody re-runs it.
